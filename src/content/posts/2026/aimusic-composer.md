# Music for Your Podcast, Stream or Indie Game: A Practical Guide for Creators Who Are Not Composers

Podcasts need intros, streams need background loops, and small games need a soundtrack. If you make things but do not write music, sourcing audio becomes a recurring puzzle. This guide walks through the options, what to check before you publish, and how to keep the result sounding like your project.

* Decide what the music needs to do before you look for it.
* Know the difference between royalty-free, licensed and generated audio.
* Check commercial-use terms for every track you publish.
* Keep loops and stingers short, editable and consistent.
* Store the license or plan details next to the audio file.

## Start with the job, not the genre

"I need some lo-fi" is a starting point, but it is not a brief. A better question is what the music has to do. A podcast intro has to signal that the show is starting and then get out of the way. A stream loop has to sit under speech for hours without becoming irritating. A game level theme has to loop cleanly and match the pace of play. Write that job down in one sentence before you start searching or generating, and it becomes much easier to reject tracks that sound nice but do not fit.

## Three ways to get music

Broadly, creators who do not compose have three routes.

### Stock and royalty-free libraries

Libraries offer finished tracks with clear licenses. The trade-off is that popular tracks turn up in many other projects, and you are choosing from what exists rather than describing what you want. Read the license carefully: "royalty-free" usually means you do not pay per play, not that there are no conditions at all.

### Commissioning a composer

Working with a composer gives you something unique and a real collaborator who understands your project. It costs more and takes longer, but for a game's main theme or a long-running show's identity, it is often worth it. A good brief, with references and the one-sentence job described above, makes the collaboration smoother.

### AI generators

AI generators sit in between: you describe what you want and get a draft quickly, then iterate. They are useful for exploring directions, producing placeholder audio while a project is in progress, or creating short pieces where a commission would be overkill.

AIMusic is one browser-based [AI song and music generator](https://aimusic.so/), and it runs on desktop and mobile with nothing to install. It offers three model options, V6 mini, V6 and V6 Wild. Free accounts use V6 mini, which the site describes as "lightweight, fast". The site describes V6 as giving "greater musical expression with more natural vocals and richer details". An Instrumental switch produces backing tracks only. Commercial use of generated songs requires a paid plan, and songs made on the free tier are public.

Other services, such as Suno and Udio, take a similar prompt-to-song approach. Each one has its own plans and usage rules, so compare those terms as carefully as you compare the sound.

## Licensing is the part you cannot skip

Whichever route you choose, publishing music means you need the right to use it in that context. Before a track goes into an episode, a stream or a game build, check:

1. **Commercial use.** Monetized podcasts, sponsored streams and paid games are commercial uses. Make sure the license or plan you are on covers that.
2. **Platform rules.** Streaming and video platforms run their own content checks. Keep proof of your license handy in case a track is flagged.
3. **Visibility of your work.** Some services make free-tier creations public. If you do not want an unreleased theme visible before launch, check this before you generate.
4. **Attribution.** Some licenses require credit in show notes or game credits. Note the exact wording required.

A simple habit helps here: save a small text file next to every audio file with the source, the date, and the plan or license it was obtained under. When a question comes up months later, you will have the answer in one place.

## Make it fit your project

Getting a track is only half the work. A few editing steps make generic audio feel like it belongs.

**Trim to the job.** A podcast intro rarely needs more than a short phrase before the host starts talking. Cut at a musical point, such as the end of a bar, and add a short fade so the cut does not feel abrupt.

**Build stingers from the same material.** Take a short section of your theme for transitions, ad breaks or level-complete moments. Reusing the same melody or sound makes a show or game feel coherent.

**Make loops repeat cleanly.** For streams and games, test the loop point by listening to it repeat several times. If you hear a click or a jump, adjust the start and end points or add a crossfade. In a game, also listen to the loop while actually playing the level, because a loop that sounds fine on its own can feel repetitive once you hear it for a long stretch of play.

**Leave room for voices.** Music under speech should be lower and simpler than you expect. Pulling down the midrange slightly, where most speech sits, helps voices stay clear without turning the music off.

## Where the shortcuts fall short

Every route has limits. Library tracks can be overused. Commissions need time and budget. Generated drafts can be uneven, so expect to try several times and edit what you keep, and remember that describing a sound in words is a skill that takes practice. Terms for all three can change, so the plan details you saved are worth re-reading before a big launch.

## A small first project

If you have been putting off giving your project a sound, start small. Write the one-sentence job for a single intro or menu theme, try one option from each route, and pick the one that fits best. Save the license note next to the file, trim it to length, and use it for a few weeks. You will learn more about what your project needs from living with one track than from auditioning dozens.
