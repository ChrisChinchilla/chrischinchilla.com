In 2017, I gave a presentation at a Write the Docs event about interacting with documentation via a chat interface. At the time, "old-style" chatbots were all the rage, with companies attempting to automate actions via Facebook Messenger, Telegram, and other apps and custom chat interfaces.

I proposed a chatbot (Docsbot) that could gather context from a user's system and chat history, then browse documentation and related content to provide direct, helpful answers to questions.

People weren't convinced. Because at the time, bots, frankly, sucked. They were language trees with pattern matching, and you frequently ended up in conversations that went nowhere or resulted in "I'm sorry, I don't understand" responses.

However, at the back of my mind, I noticed and knew that all people ever really wanted to do was ask questions and get answers. Look at Google search. Look at online communities such as Stack Overflow. Look at the endless supply of people frustrated in communities by "drive-by questions" that others have answered many times before.

All anyone ever really wanted was an answer to a question, and for many years, we documentarians found various ways to facilitate that and make it easier. While efforts such as search, use case-based information architecture (IA), and related links made us feel like we were helping, I often wonder if they ever really did. For countless years, internal and external stakeholders complained about documentation and not being able to find what they were looking for, and I wonder now if all we were ever doing was rearranging the deck chairs on the Titanic.

Fast-forward ten years, and now I feel fairly safe in saying my point has been proved. The current "AI" craze may be a bubble fit to burst, but like any other technology trend, elements of it will remain, and I think constrained knowledge distillation will be one of them.

Using AI agents and interfaces to interact with documentation isn't perfect, but we can't deny what many of our users have always wanted, just because it changes the way we work. That would be disingenuous. The work of a documentarian has changed and will continue to change. We are likely to do less actual writing and more work ensuring that the information that goes into AI systems and the information that comes out is as accurate and up to date as possible. I don't think I am the first person to use this term, but I am going to try and popularise it. We are now more "context engineers".
